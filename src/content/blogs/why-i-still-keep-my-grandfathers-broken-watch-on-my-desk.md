---
title: "Why Do I Still Keep My Grandfather's Broken Watch on My Desk?"
date: June 14th, 2026
---

My grandfather died when I was nineteen. He was the kind of person who fixed things before replacing them: radios, chairs, bicycle chains, and once my school bag strap with thread that outlasted the bag itself. After he passed, my mother gave me his pocket watch. It had stopped years earlier; the glass was scratched; the chain was tangled. I put it on my desk because throwing it away felt wrong and fixing it felt impossible.

For months I did nothing with it except move it aside when I opened notebooks. Then, during my first year of diploma and my first serious week of programming at 17, I started noticing when I moved it. Always before hard problems. Always before exams. Not prayer; just placement. Like setting a bookmark in the room.

So, I present my analysis on the topic at multiple different levels of depth.

# Level One: Sentimental Logic

At first glance, the logic seems simple. Broken watch → no utility → store or discard.

But objects tied to people are not evaluated on utility alone. They are evaluated on **presence cost**:

```
presence_cost = memory_intensity × daily_visibility
utility = 0 (watch does not tick)
keep? = presence_cost > clutter_cost
```

In my case, clutter cost was low. The watch is small. Memory intensity was high. **Keeping it was rational even if the mechanism was dead.**

# Level Two: What the Watch Actually Does

The watch does not tell time. It tells sequence.

When I look at it before debugging, I remember a person who believed most problems are fixable if you open them carefully and do not panic at the first broken piece. That is absurdly relevant when your code throws:

```
Exception in thread "main" ...
```

at 1:12 AM.

![His pocket watch, still stopped](/blogs/cartoon-watch.svg)

I once tried to get it repaired at a small shop near home. The shopkeeper opened the back, shrugged, and said the spring was "tired." I laughed because it sounded like something my grandfather would have said about people, not machines.

# Level Three: Missing Someone vs Remembering Someone

People ask whether missing him gets quieter over time. My answer is poorly behaved: **yes in volume, no in specificity.**

Early grief is loud and constant. Later grief is selective. It shows up on random Tuesdays when I smell chai a certain way, or when I ship something small and think he would have asked to see it twice.

The watch does not reduce grief. It gives grief a location. That sounds sentimental until you realize engineers also use anchors: logs, commits, screenshots, notes. The watch is my offline anchor.

# Level Four: Should I Fix It?

_At this point I have already lost the original question and am extending the argument until it barely resembles a repair decision, but I think it is still interesting._

Fixing the watch would be satisfying. Keeping it broken is also satisfying, because the object remains exactly as he left it in my memory: patient, worn, still worth keeping.

Maybe I will repair it someday. Maybe I will not. Either way, it stays on my desk next to my laptop, which is the clock I actually use.

When people ask why it is there, I say: "It reminds me to start carefully."

That is the whole reason.

Take that, tired spring.
